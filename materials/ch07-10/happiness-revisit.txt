L41@P2: ABSTRACT
L42@P2: The Sustainable Happiness Model (SHM) has been influential in positive psychology and well-being
L43@P2: science. However, the ‘pie chart’ aspect of the model has received valid critiques. In this article, we
L44@P2: start by agreeing with many such critiques, while also explaining the context of the original article
L45@P2: and noting that we were speculative but not dogmatic therein. We also show that subsequent
L46@P2: research has supported the most important premise of the SHM – namely, that individuals can
L47@P2: boost their well-being via their intentional behaviors, and maintain that boost in the longer-term.
L48@P2: However, such effects may be weaker than we initially believed. We describe three contemporary
L49@P2: models descended from the thinking embodied in the SHM – the Eudaimonic Activity Model, the
L50@P2: Hedonic Adaptation Prevention model, and the Positive Activity Model. Research testing these
L51@P2: models has further supported the premise that how people live makes a difference for their wellbeing.

L61@P2: In a widely cited article, Lyubomirsky, Sheldon, and Schkade
L62@P2: (2005) proposed a heuristic framework for understanding
L63@P2: the influences on well-being. The Sustainable Happiness
L64@P2: Model, as illustrated in the now well-known pie chart, distinguished among three overlapping kinds of influences:
L65@P2: inherent genetic predispositions, current life circumstances,
L66@P2: and current intentional activities. Lyubomirsky et al. also
L67@P2: provided, based on certain starting assumptions and a nonexhaustive review of the literature of that time, initial estimates concerning the relative importance of the three
L68@P2: factors in impacting chronic happiness levels: approximately 50% for genetic factors, 10% for circumstantial factors, and the remaining 40% for volitional or intentional
L69@P2: activity factors. Figure 1 illustrates this basic pie chart.
L70@P2: Based on their review, Lyubomirsky, Sheldon et al. (2005)
L71@P2: suggested that there is considerable potential for people to
L72@P2: take action to influence their own happiness. If happiness is
L73@P2: not fully determined by a person’s genetics and circumstances, then there must be something left over for intentional behavior. At the time, these conclusions supported
L74@P2: the nascent science of positive psychology, helping to
L75@P2: justify its search for new ways to help people activate
L76@P2: their potentials. The conclusions also dovetailed well with
L77@P2: Thomas Jefferson’s contention that the right to ‘pursue
L78@P2: happiness’ must be foundational in a just society, and
L79@P2: were well aligned with Western and individualist ideological assumptions more generally.
L80@P2: Today, however, the pie chart diagram appears to have
L81@P2: outlived its usefulness (for recent critical reviews, see
L82@P2: Bergink, 2015; Brown & Rohrer, 2019; Kashdan, 2015;
L83@P2: Krueger, 2015). Brown and Rohrer (2019) have provided
L84@P2: the most elaborated analysis, especially of the initial percentage estimates we provided. These critiques, with which
L85@P2: we mostly agree, have provided us with an opportunity to
L86@P2: articulate our current thinking. However, rather than
L87@P2: addressing such criticisms in detail here, in this article we
L88@P2: take a broader perspective. Accordingly, we first revisit the
L89@P2: context in which the chart was proposed, point out the
L90@P2: cautiousness with which we originally proposed it, and
L91@P2: remind readers of our original goal in proposing it – namely,
L92@P2: to show that it is theoretically possible for people to influence their own happiness via their intentional behaviors.
L93@P2: Our reasoning was that if happiness is not completely
L94@P2: determined by one’s genetic endowment (which is, after
L95@P2: all, relatively constant over time), then happiness must
L96@P2: fluctuate over time (as it clearly does). We further argued
L97@P2: that patterns of behavioral activity provide one logical
L98@P2: source of influence upon those fluctuations, and perhaps
L99@P2: the most important influence, given the relatively weak
L100@P2: effects that had been observed at that time within mainly
L101@P2: Western cultures for many demographic-type variables,
L102@P2: such as income, marital status, gender, and ethnicity.

L169@P4: that intentional behavior can make a difference –
